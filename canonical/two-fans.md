# Two distinct high fans

<a id="C"></a>

~~~text
         high triangle component             safe head matchings at exceptional books

            x ── b ── c ── z                  head ab uses ax and bc
              \  |    |  /                    head ac uses az and bc
                   a
                                                only possible conflict here is bc
       abx — abc — acz

    In the auxiliary multigraph the two exceptional book vertices are ab and ac.
    No auxiliary edge joins them. Deleting them leaves ordinary trees:

              ab —— [good tree T] —— ac
                   at most one attachment to each exceptional book
~~~

**Theorem.** Every finite simple graph with at most one ordinary high fan satisfies Tuza. Here every fan vertex has degree at least eight in the whole input, and additional edges among them are permitted when counting fan copies. Thus every violation has at least two distinct seven-edge fan subgraphs in its high layer. The fans may overlap.

The at-most-one condition is hereditary, so take a subgraph-minimal violation. There must be one high fan by the earlier occurrence theorem. Its three triangles form an entire shared-edge component of the high triangles. All other components are books, isolated faces or tetrahedra. Distinct high tetrahedra share at most one vertex; a high tetrahedron and the five fan vertices also share at most one vertex. Indeed every pair of tetrahedron vertices is adjacent, the fan support is induced, every edge within it is on a fan face, and the high tetrahedron's faces are a separate entire component. An outside high sees at most one corner of a tetrahedron. [Full component classification and uniqueness convention](fan-structure.md#component)

**The exceptional low vertices.** The earlier local argument for a fan-free high layer carries over once the seven-rim issue below is checked. A high $K_5$ missing at most one edge and a high octahedron each contain more than one fan; the high square wheel has four. These forbid the same five- and six-link cases and the private-high case of a wide seven–seven square. Sixes would have four high neighbors inducing a tetrahedron and two nonadjacent sevens seeing it. The wide square pair closes and reduces. A wide clique pair forces both endpoints' high neighborhoods to be the very same tetrahedron and all their private vertices to see it.

Call a seven normal if it sees no high tetrahedron. It cannot neighbor a six, and the only surviving wide seven pair would make both endpoints see a tetrahedron. Each five-neighbor is narrow by subsumption. Therefore all its wide spokes go high. On the verified domain of 77 candidate complete seven-links, the recorded check counted ordinary high fans directly by choosing a high center and an oriented path of four high rim vertices in its neighborhood, accepting just one reversal per path. Of 159 markings having every wide spoke high, five have zero fans, none have one, two or three, and nine have four. The zero markings are exactly the previous four $FEzvO$ rows and one $FFzvO$ row. Thus **every normal seven has one of the five old marked rims**, even when an exterior seven tips one or both shared fan edges. In particular no normal seven sees the entire fan. A seven seeing a tetrahedron cannot see the entire fan either, since their union has at least eight high vertices. At a seven seeing a tetrahedron $Q$ without a wide seven partner, the old argument and 194 local certificates remove all but the $rs$-present, disjoint-two-corner row. All its uses of the one-corner bound on an outside high remain valid. This row would force $r$ to be a seven sharing two corners of $Q$; its common-neighbor triple with the original root would contain the low $s$, contrary to the five normal markings, and a special $r$ cannot see a different tetrahedron containing the two corners. The earlier proof first excludes the five possibility at these slots by the necessary high subsumer triple. All five-neighbor and terminal component arguments transfer. Hence no fives, sixes or sevens seeing a tetrahedron remain. The fixed two-seven path reduction leaves the full two-book rim at every remaining seven, with the two ends of each of its two disjoint bases, and two full tips, all high. **Which books require a changed matching?** Put $U=ab,\ V=ac$. Every represented base has two distinct full high tips in the seven's actual neighborhood. A tetrahedron edge cannot be represented, since its two tips would force the seven to see that tetrahedron. The only repeated high edges of the fan component are $U,V$. Call any represented base outside them good. Its book is a separate high triangle component, with every true side lying on exactly one high triangle in the entire graph. Sides from different good books are distinct and avoid all seven fan edges. A seven representing $U$ must see $a,b,x,c$; the fixed matching $ax,bc$ is available. At $V$ the analogous matching is $az,bc$. Either choice avoids $U,V$ and every good book. The two choices share $bc$, so the proof uses at most one of these special matchings in any one reduction.

**Cycles and small good books.** The auxiliary multigraph has the represented bases as vertices and *every* seven as one edge joining its two distinct, vertex-disjoint bases. Thus there are no loops and no edge $UV$. On an oriented cycle containing at most one exceptional base, use the triangle from each seven to its tail base and two triangles through a matching of sides of its head. Use the just-given safe matching if the head is exceptional. The remaining heads belong to different good books. All packed edges are distinct, including on a parallel-edge cycle. Buy all cycle bases, all head matchings and all three tip spokes per root; this is at most six edges per root for three packed triangles. All triangles at a deleted root are hit; all packed edges surviving root deletion were bought. Thus every cycle would have to contain **both** exceptional book vertices.

Let $B$ be a good book of auxiliary degree one or two. At one of its incident sevens choose any of its four sides $f$ using a full high tip. If the degree is one, orient the one-edge path $A\to B$. If the degree is two, orient $A\to B\to C$, with the indicated root as the first edge. The other bases $A,C$ differ: parallel $AB$ edges would be a cycle with at most one exceptional base. Select a matching at head $B$ containing $f$, and, when needed, any matching at head $C$, using its safe matching if it is exceptional. Only one head is potentially exceptional. A tail at the other exceptional base is harmless because the special matching avoids *both* repeated fan edges. All chosen sides are distinct. For a path of $k=1,2$ roots buy its $k+1$ bases, all $3k$ tip spokes, and the $2k-1$ used sides other than $f$. This is at most $6k$ purchases for $3k$ packed triangles. In addition, delete $f$ from the residual. Its unique high page uses the purchased $B$. Hence a violation requires a seven outside the path seeing $f$. This path uses every seven incident to $B$.

At an outside seven such a good side can never be a repeated base, nor a true side of an exceptional book: all high pages on that book belong to the isolated fan. If it is a true side of another good book, its unique high triangle identifies the same repeated base $B$, making the seven an incident user already included. The only possible role is the unique dangling high edge of its full rim. The four different $f$'s therefore consume four different sevens, with distinct demands across all small good books. Adjacent roots introduce no packing collision, since packed spokes always lead from their corresponding root to a high vertex.

**Degree count after removing $U,V$.** Remove whichever of $U,V$ are represented. The induced graph on good books is a forest since any cycle there would reduce. Any one of its nonempty tree components $T$ has at most one attachment edge to each exceptional vertex: two such edges, together with their path in $T$, make a cycle with at most one exceptional book, also when the two attachments are parallel. Let $r\le2$ be its number of attachment edges and $L,D,J$ count its good vertices of degree one, two, and at least three **in the full auxiliary graph**. There are $k=L+D+J$ vertices in $T$, and their full degrees sum to $2(k-1)+r$. Thus $J\le L+r-2\le L$. In particular $L+D\ge1$, including when $T$ is a singleton. The number of root edges assigned to this component by counting its internal and incident edges is
$$
       n_T=k-1+r\le 2L+D+1\le3(L+D).
$$
Every root is assigned once, since no edge joins the two exceptional vertices. If any seven exists, there is at least one good tree. The witnesses already established require at least $4\sum_T(L+D)$ sevens, exceeding their total. Thus none exists.

**All-high terminal.** In the fan pack $abx,acz$, and buy $ab,ac$. Each separate tetrahedron contributes one packed face for two purchases, and each book or singleton contributes one packed triangle for one purchase. There are no intercomponent edge conflicts. This contradicts a violation and completes the hereditary theorem.

**Boundary examples.** One connected minimum-degree-seven graph has all seven fan edges externally tipped, and two sevens with the two different exceptional bases in their full rims. They see $a,b,c,x$ and $a,b,c,z$ respectively, and neither sees all five. The two safe head matchings really do compete for $bc$ in this graph. Its high layer has exactly one ordinary fan; the entire graph has 49 vertices, 289 edges and 35 triangles, with $\nu=\tau=10$. A second example has an outside seven seeing $a,b,c$ but neither $x$ nor $z$. All fan edges again have exterior triangles; it has 57 vertices, 369 edges, 10 triangles and $(\nu,\tau)=(5,6)$. Both graphs were checked from their actual edges, with two independent path counts for the marked-rim census and literal packing and cover certificates.
